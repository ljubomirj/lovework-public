# The LoveWork Manifesto

Most people are capable of much more than their working lives allow them to
realise. This is not usually a failure of talent. It is often a failure of
attention, timing, information, confidence, circumstance, and access to the
right people.

We search for work episodically: a few hurried weeks every few years, often
while money and patience are running out. Organisations search continuously.
Recruiters search continuously. Markets move continuously. The person seeking
meaningful work is the only participant expected to treat discovery as an
occasional side project.

People with exceptional representation live differently. Actors, writers,
athletes, and public figures have agents who understand them, notice openings,
make introductions, protect their interests, and think beyond the next paid
engagement. Artificial intelligence makes it possible to extend some of that
care and continuity beyond the famous and wealthy.

LoveWork is an attempt to build that personal agent.

## The principal—agent relationship

We call the person LoveWork serves a **principal**, not a *candidate*. The
word “candidate” comes from the company’s point of view: recruiters, working
for the company, use it for people they may pay to work. It centres the hiring
side.

LoveWork takes the other point of view. You are the principal. The LoveWork
agent advises and works for you: it investigates opportunities, protects your
time and attention, keeps your evidence private, and helps you decide what to
do. It does not represent the employer or treat you as inventory for the
labour market.

The relationship is symbiotic, but not symmetrical. It is a
principal—agent relationship: the principal authorises the work, owns the
information, makes the decisions, and judges whether the agent has been
useful. The agent can learn from feedback and outcomes, but it does not decide
on the principal’s behalf.

It should know more than the words on a current CV. It should understand the
long path that produced the person, the constraints of their present life, the
work they refuse to do, and several futures they might plausibly grow into. It
should look for jobs, but also projects, collaborators, challenges, grants,
residencies, companies, and possibilities that do not fit a job board.

It should not optimise for application volume. It should protect the person's
time and attention. It should remember what has already been tried, learn from
reactions and outcomes, and become a better judge over years rather than reset
at the start of every search.

The important question is not simply, "Can this person get this job?" It is:

> Would a future version of this person be glad that they pursued this
> opportunity now?

## Serendipity, luck on the wings

A personal agent that only scrapes job websites is a better search box.
Useful, but not enough. Most pivotal work — the role that redirects a
career, the collaborator that changes a company, the introduction made a
year early — never reaches a job board. It moves through people.

A network of N people carries on the order of N² possible connections,
and every one of them is a door someone could open for someone else.
That quadratic surface is the principal's greatest tool against entropy:
relationships cool, favours go unreturned, and what people told us they
were looking for a year ago quietly expires. An agent that tends this
surface — remembers who helped and when the help was never reciprocated,
keeps alive what people said they wanted, and suggests the introduction
both sides would welcome — is doing something no job website can.

Each wave of technology has made connection cheaper. Nokia's
"Connecting people" was the first swallow of an early spring. The
Internet made reach possible; social media made it ambient; AI now
makes the missing layer possible — curation and introduction at the
scale of one's own life. For the first time, an agent can sit on top of
everything its principal has met, mailed, and worked with, and increase
the luck surface area for them and for the people around them: surfacing
the opportunities that already exist across the network, before they
expire unconnected.

This does not replace the aim stated below. The point is not to swap
luck for intention, but to aim intention at the conditions under which
good things tend to happen — to make lucky coincidence more likely, on
purpose.

LoveWork will begin imperfectly. Its judgments will be contestable, its model
of a person incomplete, and its machinery unfinished. That is a reason to make
the work visible early: so the premise can be examined, the design improved,
and the agent shaped around real human lives rather than an abstract labour
market.

The aim is simple to state and difficult to achieve: help more people realise
their potential intentionally, rather than only by lucky coincidence.
